## 1. The brief

Gareth and his wife have begun sponsoring a child through World Vision UK. Gareth's brief, condensed from his words
of 19 October 2026:

- **What it is.** An emotive plea for child sponsorship, given freely to the charity as an advert it can share on
  social media, to bring many new sponsors. It is not about Gareth and his wife.
- **How it should feel.** Emotionally devastating. It starts from the gut-wrenching truth of what these children
  endure, and it connects viewers with their own guilt for standing by and doing nothing.
- **Where it goes.** It builds to a glorious and fruitful future where no child is left behind, and where poverty,
  skin colour and country of origin become meaningless. It moves from desolation to the bright, hopeful welcome of
  God's kingdom.
- **The faith.** It is a Christian charity and a Christian message: love, peace, reconciliation, Christ brought to
  the lost, the lonely, the broken and innocent children. It should not alienate people who are not religious, but
  it should challenge them.
- **What sponsorship means to him.** A small monthly gift; letters, art and video both ways; treasure of heart and
  mind (puzzles, trinkets, his sons' art made with love, child to child, books, learning); a visit after a year;
  and a long hope of a home away from home.
- **The sound.** A haunting live worship song with sing-spoken verses, a chorus that soars, a simple drum
  metronome, and a guitar that builds.

## 2. The idea in one paragraph

At five in the morning, before the cockerel, a seven-year-old girl walks an hour on red earth to fill a yellow can
with brown water, and walks the long way round the school she cannot go into. At seven in the morning, in a green
and pleasant land, a father's hot tap runs while his boys fight over cereal, and her face comes up on his phone.
He scrolls past her, three times, and a cockerel cries. Then the film turns: she looks up out of the screen, straight
at us, and the chorus says what the Gospel says, that the face we scrolled past was Christ's. A child's voice asks,
"Will you sponsor me?", and he answers with the godparent's vow: "With the help of God, I will." A pound a day goes
into the ground, as a well, a nurse and a classroom roof, and a padded envelope crosses the sea: a boy's felt-tip
lion, stickers and crayons. Twelve weeks later her drawing comes back, a house, a blue door, a sun with a face and a
tree. The children's drawings start to come alive in the painted world. Her drawn tree takes root and grows into a
tree over a table as long as the sky, laid for every nation, where every face is lit by the same warm light and a
chair beside her has your name on it. The film ends where it began, at five in the morning. The cockerel just means
morning now. The yellow can is full beside the door, and she is running to school with a book.

## 3. What the design guards against

Gareth asked for devastation and guilt, so the design has to decide where they come from. These rules are written
so that World Vision can share the film, and so that it does what it is for.

1. **Dignity, not pity.** The truth of her life is shown through things and distances: the can, the hour each way,
   the brown water, her brother's cough heard at night, the school seen from the road. It is never shown through her
   suffering body. Charity codes on images and messages (Dóchas in Ireland, Bond in the UK) ask for dignity, and
   World Vision's own child-safe guidelines protect sponsored children's privacy and dignity. A film that breaks
   those codes is unlikely to be shared by the charity it was made for.
2. **The guilt is ours.** Devastation comes from the cut between her morning and ours, and the guilt from the scroll:
   the viewer is shown doing, a second ago, exactly what the singer confesses. Nothing is squeezed out of her
   suffering. Guilt alone can make people look away. Here it turns at once into an action a person can take (the
   vow) and a picture they can want (the table).
3. **No saviour.** The change is made by her own community, the well-diggers, the nurse and the teacher. The sponsor's
   part is a pound a day and an envelope. Nobody flies in and nobody is rescued. The sponsor's family is seen only
   as silhouettes and hands.
4. **Christian in its own voice, help for every child.** The film's prayer, its Gospel lines and its table are
   plainly Christian, and they are aimed at the viewer. The girl is never preached to, and her help never depends on
   faith. World Vision describes itself as faith-inspired and open to all, and holds a policy against proselytism,
   which it defines as using coercion or inducement to make people listen to a religious message before helping
   them. The kingdom in this film is the table where everyone is welcome.
5. **Only true promises.** A new sponsor must be able to do everything the film shows. So the money goes to the
   community, not to her hand. The treasure fits an A4 padded envelope. The first letter takes twelve weeks. There
   are no visits to her home, and no journey to the UK. Every factual line is in the song's record of claims.
6. **The real child stays private.** The girl in the film is invented. She does not have the name, the face, the
   village, the letters or the drawings of the child Gareth's family sponsors. Gareth's family appears nowhere.
7. **Clearly painted, never photographic.** No viewer should mistake an invented child for a real one, so the
   film's people are painted. The same goes for any generated still used as reference.
8. **The charity's name and logo are theirs to give.** The end card carries World Vision's name, logo or web address
   only with World Vision's agreement (section 14).

## 4. Tone rules

- **She always has agency.** She carries, walks, draws, writes, laughs and runs. She looks at us once, steadily, and
  never pleads.
- **She never cries.** The film's tears are on our side of the world.
- **No body as evidence.** No flies, no swollen bellies, no torn clothes in close-up, no dirt on her face for effect.
- **No costume clichés.** Clothes are ordinary and mended, not "tribal". The village is a place people live in,
  with a cockerel, a fence, a door and a path, not a backdrop of poverty.
- **One face acts: hers.** Everyone else is a silhouette that emotes, with one accessory each (memory:
  silhouettes emote).
- **The UK house is never mocked.** The comfort is shown plainly. The father is ordinary and so are we.
- **Hope is earned.** Nothing gets brighter until the vow in the first chorus. The table is not shown until the
  bridge.
- **The table has no hierarchy.** It has no head, no host's chair and no servers. Every face gets the same light.
- **The last picture is a child running to school,** not the table. The kingdom's sign is a child's ordinary morning.

## 5. The structure: eight movements

The song's sections map onto eight movements. Times come with the take.

| # | Section | Movement | Light | What the picture does |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | Intro, verse 1 | **Before the cockerel** | pre-dawn indigo to grey first light | her road, the can, the water, the school she passes |
| II | Verse 2 | **Green and pleasant land** | cold grey-green window, warm kitchen, the phone's blue-white | our morning, matched object by object to hers; the phone; the scroll |
| III | Pre-chorus | **Three times** | the phone's glow, then dark | three scrolls; the cockerel's cry over the dawn |
| IV | Chorus 1 | **Was You, looking at me** | first gold light, from behind her | she looks out of the screen at us; the vow |
| V | Verse 3, her letter | **A pound a day** | full morning, paper white | the well, the nurse, the roof; the envelope's journey; her drawing comes back |
| VI | Chorus 2 | **The drawings come alive** | warm day | the children's felt-tip world bleeds into the painted one |
| VII | Bridge, final chorus | **The table** | dawn gold to full light, key lift | the drawn tree takes root; the table as long as the sky; the empty chair; *we will* |
| VIII | Outro | **Five in the morning** | the opening's pre-dawn, now warm | the opening shot repeated: the can full by the door, she runs to school |

The arc is light. The film starts in the dark before dawn, turns in the chorus, and ends in a dawn that is just a
morning. A low drone of brown and indigo gives way, chorus by chorus, to the gold of the table.

### Medium

There are two media, and they meet.

1. **The world** is painted: watercolour washes and broken-colour oil on warm paper, from `_medium.ts` (CRAFT.md §2).
   Act I is the driest and most granular, with heavy paper grain, washes that barely cover the paper, and lost edges
   into the dark. The UK kitchen is smoother and glossier: comfort has a sheen.
2. **The drawings** are the children's: felt-tip strokes with their streaky overlaps, wax crayon with its grain
   skipping over the paper, flat bright colour, wobbly lines. They start on paper. From verse 3 they leak into the
   world: the felt-tip lion walks off the page, the stickers become stars, and her drawn tree takes root in the
   painted earth. By the final chorus the two media are one picture.

The drawing medium is new kit work: a felt-tip renderer and a crayon-grain texture in `_medium.ts`.

### Light

- **I.** Ambient deep and cool. The key light is a thin grey line of dawn low behind her, and she is lit mostly from
  the sky.
- **II.** Two light sources. The window's cold daylight, and the phone's blue-white light on the father's hands.
- **IV.** The first warm key light, from behind her, rimming her hair at the first "Holy".
- **VII.** One warm light over everyone at the table. The ambient lifts until shadows are coloured, never black.

## 7. Motifs and visual rhymes

1. **The road at five in the morning** is the visual rhyme. The film opens and closes on the same shot. First she
   is carrying water. At the end she is running to school, and the can is full by the door.
2. **The cockerel.** On the fence in the first shot, silent. It cries at the end of the pre-chorus, over the dawn,
   the moment of denial. In the last shot it crows again, and it only means morning.
3. **The yellow can** is the accent. A burden in I, a matched shot against the hot tap in II, standing full beside
   her door in VIII.
4. **The scroll.** The feed's vertical motion carries the cuts in II and III. It returns reversed in VII, when the
   camera climbs the tree and the table rises toward the sky.
5. **Shoes and feet.** Her bare feet in the red dust match two pairs of school shoes by a UK door. At the table every
   child's feet are bare in the grass, and nobody is wearing shoes.
6. **The drawings.** The boy's felt-tip lion goes out to her, and her house, blue door, sun with a face and tree come
   back. Each comes alive in turn. The tree is the fruitful future, and it grows from a drawing (the mustard seed:
   Matthew 13:31–32).
7. **The chair.** The empty chair beside her at the table, with a blank card in crayon. It is the viewer's.

## 9. Camera and cut grammar

- **I.** Wide lock-offs, held long by this film's standards. Her footsteps land on the metronome kick. The camera
  doesn't move: the distance is the point.
- **II.** Tight, cluttered and close, at table height. Match cuts from her world to his: the can to the hot tap, the
  brown water to the cereal milk, bare feet to the school shoes, the far school to the two lunchboxes.
- **III.** The scroll. Each "scrolled on by" is a vertical whip of the feed. Her face passes three times, a beat
  shorter each time. After the third, cut to the dark and the cockerel's cry.
- **IV.** One slow push-in to her eyes as she looks up out of the screen. This is the film's only push straight to a
  face. On the vow it stops and holds.
- **V.** Lateral travel. The envelope's journey goes left to right across the map: kitchen, letterbox, postbox,
  aircraft, red road, her hands. Her drawing comes back right to left.
- **VI.** Handheld-free floats, with drawings drifting through the painted world.
- **VII.** A crane up the trunk of the tree, the scroll reversed, then the long table from above, then down to the
  chair.
- **VIII.** The opening shot exactly, then she runs out of frame.

**Transition families.** There are four (CRAFT.md §8): the match cut on objects (II), the scroll wipe (III, reversed
in VII), the paper fold, in which the envelope's fold carries us between worlds (V), and hard cuts on the beat. No
dissolves except the felt-tip bleeding into the world, which is a medium change, not a cut.

**Restraint.** No shakes in the whole film. One flash at most: the sun cresting over the table on the key lift. The
beat before the first "Holy" is silent and gets nothing.

## 13. Words on screen, lengths and formats

- **Muted viewing.** Much social video is watched with the sound off, and the film's turn is in its words. So,
  unlike *God Moved*, this film should show its lyric, inside the concept: his lines small and quiet at the foot of
  the frame, her letter in her handwriting, and the chorus's "Will you sponsor me?" in a child's crayon (DIRECTION.md
  §4 for readability). Decision owed.
- **The master.** The full song at 4K60, 16:9, for YouTube and the charity's website.
- **The cutdowns,** from the master's own plates:
  - **60 s:** verse 2, the pre-chorus and chorus 1. It runs from the kitchen through the scroll and the turn to the
    vow, then the end card.
  - **30 s:** the third scroll, the cockerel, the first "Holy" and her look, the vow, the table's key lift, the end

  for 9:16 rather than cropped. It is new kit work: a second camera per plate, planned in the plates table.
