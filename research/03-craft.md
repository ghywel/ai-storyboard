## A character who acts (DIRECTION §6)

- **Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston**, *Disney Animation: The Illusion of Life*.
  - Abbeville Press, 1981. Reissued by Hyperion in 1995 as *The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation*.
  - Its twelve principles include squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, exaggeration and appeal.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_Animation:_The_Illusion_of_Life;
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation. [R], secondary.
- **John Lasseter**, "Principles of Traditional Animation Applied to 3D Computer Animation", SIGGRAPH '87, Computer
  Graphics 21(4), 35–44. Source:
  https://history.siggraph.org/learning/principles-of-traditional-animation-applied-to-3d-computer-animation-by-lasseter/ [R]

## Manga emotion symbols, lines of force and the chibi shift (DIRECTION §6)

- **Neil Cohn and Sean Ehly**, "The vocabulary of manga: Visual morphology in dialects of Japanese Visual Language",
  *Journal of Pragmatics* 92 (2016), 17–29.
  - It catalogues 73 symbols: the anger vein, the giant sweat drop, gloom lines, sparkles, zoom (focus) lines, and the
    sudden switch to chibi ("superdeformation", a suppletion that signals overwhelming emotion).
  - The best single citation for the kit in `engine/src/scenes/_manga.ts`.
  - Source: https://www.visuallanguagelab.com/P/NCSE_mangamorphology.pdf [R]
- **Neil Cohn**, *The Visual Language of Comics*, Bloomsbury Academic, 2013. It covers upfixes (p. 42), motion lines
  (p. 108) and Japanese Visual Language (ch. 8). Source:
  https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/the-visual-language-of-comics-introduction-to-the-structure-and-c/ [R]
- **Neil Cohn, Beena Murthy and Tom Foulsham**, "Meaning above the head", *Journal of Cognitive Psychology* 28(5)
  (2016).
  - Readers expect an upfix above the head, agreeing with the face.
  - In Cohn's terms an upfix floats above (hearts, steam, ?). The vein and the sweat drop sit on the head.
  - Source: https://visuallanguagelab.com/P/NCBMTF_Upfixes.pdf [R]
- **Neil Cohn and Stephen Maher**, "The notion of the motion", *Brain Research* 1601 (2015), 73–84. Motion lines
  speed comprehension, and reversed lines slow it. Source:
  https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/the-notion-of-the-motion-the-neurocognition-of-motion-lines-in-vi [R]
- **Takekuma Kentarō**, on keiyu, in *Manga no yomikata* (with Natsume Fusanosuke), Takarajimasha, 1995.
  - Manpu are the stand-alone symbols. Focus and speed lines are "effects".
  - English translation by Karen Curtin and Satomi Newsom (2024):
    https://www.japanesestudies.org.uk/ejcjs/vol24/iss3/curtin.html [R]
- **Mort Walker**, *The Lexicon of Comicana* (1980): the Western symbol names, such as plewds and emanata. Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lexicon_of_Comicana [R], secondary.
- **Super Deformed**: Bandai's name for Koji Yokoi's SD Gundam capsule-toy line, June 1985. "Chibi" is an ordinary
  Japanese word for a small person. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SD_Gundam;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibi_(style) [R], secondary.

## Simple figures and one face (DIRECTION §§6–7)

- **Scott McCloud**, *Understanding Comics*, Tundra, 1993.
  - "Amplification through simplification" (p. 30) and the masking effect (pp. 42–44), with Hergé as the example.
    Readers become the simple figure and see the realistic one as "other".
  - The term "ligne claire" is Joost Swarte's (1977), not McCloud's.
  - The masking effect has critics.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understanding_Comics; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masking_(comics);
    http://www.paulgravett.com/articles/article/herge_the_clear_line [R], secondary.

## Boards before production, and notes (METHOD phase 7)

- **Storyboards.** Disney credits storyman Webb Smith with the idea, early 1930s: sketches pinned in order on boards.
  There are rival accounts (Ted Sears at Fleischer; 1920s story sketches). Sources:
  https://d23.com/a-to-z/storyboards/; https://d23.com/a-to-z/smith-webb/ [R]
- **Ed Catmull with Amy Wallace**, *Creativity, Inc.*, Random House, 2014.
  - Pixar's Braintrust gives candid notes that the director may take or leave.
  - Sources: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/216369/creativity-inc-by-ed-catmull-with-amy-wallace/;
    https://longreads.com/2014/03/13/the-secret-behind-pixars-storytelling-process/ [R]

## Colour planned scene by scene (DIRECTION §2)

- **Amid Amidi**, *The Art of Pixar: The Complete Colorscripts*, Chronicle Books, 2011.
  - Ralph Eggleston brought colour scripts to Pixar with *Toy Story*.
  - Pixar did not invent them, but it was the first studio to make one for every film.
  - Lou Romano's colour scripts for *The Incredibles* were digital, in flat colour.
  - Sources: https://www.awn.com/animationworld/book-review-art-pixar; https://hyperallergic.com/the-art-of-pixar-chronicle-books/;
    https://www.thunderchunky.co.uk/articles/pixar-colour-and-tent-poles-with-lou-romano/ [R]
- **Josef Albers**, *Interaction of Color*, Yale University Press, 1963: a colour reads relative to its neighbours.
  Source: https://news.yale.edu/2023/02/17/book-color-never-fades [R]

## Planted details and staying on model (DIRECTION §5, METHOD phase 5)

- **Anton Chekhov**, letter to A. S. Lazarev (Gruzinsky), 1 November 1889: a loaded rifle must not stand on the stage
  if no one means to fire it.
  - The "gun on the wall in act one" wording comes from memoirs (Gurlyand, 1904).
  - Source: https://berlin.wolf.ox.ac.uk/lists/quotations/quotations_by_ib.html [R]
- **Model sheets.** Disney's Character Model Department was set up in late 1937 under Joe Grant. Model sheets went to
  as many as 30 artists on one character, to keep it "on model".
  - Sources: https://d23.com/walt-disney-and-the-creation-of-the-character-model-department/;
    https://www.waltdisney.org/blog/movie-models-and-miniatures [R]
  - The frozen shared kit plays the same role.

## Type that acts, and the lyric video (DIRECTION §§3–4)

- **Johnny C. Lee, Jodi Forlizzi and Scott E. Hudson**, "The Kinetic Typography Engine", UIST 2002. It applies
  squash, stretch and anticipation to text, after Thomas and Johnston and Lasseter. Sources:
  https://doi.org/10.1145/571985.571997; http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~johnny/kt/dist/files/Kinetic_Typography.pdf [R]
- **Saul Bass**: title sequences from *The Man with the Golden Arm* (1955), *Vertigo*, *North by Northwest* and
  *Psycho*. A pioneer, not "the first". Source: https://www.artofthetitle.com/designer/saul-bass/ [R]
- **Prince, "Sign o' the Times" (1987)**, directed by Bill Konersman: the lyrics only on screen. It is "widely
  considered" the first lyric video; Bob Dylan's cue cards (1965) came earlier. Source:
  https://www.grammy.com/news/list-how-much-do-you-know-about-princes-sign-o-the-times/ [R]
